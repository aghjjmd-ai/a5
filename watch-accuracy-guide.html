The Watch Accuracy Guide

No watch keeps perfect time. What matters is knowing how much drift is normal, how to measure it and when it is a sign of a problem.

01What ±s/d means

Accuracy is written in seconds per day. A watch rated −4/+6 s/d may lose up to four seconds or gain up to six in a day under test conditions. Real-world results vary with how you wear it.

02Typical accuracy by movement

MovementTypical daily rateOver a month
Everyday automatic±10 to 20 sUp to several minutes
Chronometer certified−4 to +6 sAbout 2 to 3 minutes at most
Standard quartz±0.5 sAbout 15 seconds
High-accuracy quartzUnder ±0.1 sA few seconds a year

03How to measure your watch

  1. Set it exactly to a reliable time source, to the second.
  2. Wear it as normal for at least five days.
  3. Check at the same time each day and write down the difference.
  4. Divide the total difference by the number of days.

The Accuracy Tracker does the arithmetic and compares the result with typical figures.

04Why watches drift

  • Position: a mechanical watch runs at slightly different rates dial-up, crown-down and so on.
  • Power reserve: many run faster or slower as the mainspring unwinds.
  • Temperature: extreme heat or cold changes the balance spring.
  • Magnetism: the most common cause of a sudden large gain.
  • Shock: a knock can disturb the balance.

05Using position to correct it

If your watch runs slightly fast, try leaving it dial-up overnight; if slow, crown-up. Note what happens over a few nights. Small, predictable corrections like this can keep a mechanical watch within a minute a week.

06When to see a watchmaker

Book a check if the rate changes suddenly by more than about 30 seconds a day, if the watch stops before its stated power reserve, if the date changes at the wrong time, or if moisture appears under the crystal. A demagnetising test takes minutes and solves many problems.
